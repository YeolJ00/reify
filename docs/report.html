An earlier version of this page reported three parameters, including a wooden-bowl friction of 0.317 described as the first value to land in the textbook range for wood on wood. That was an artefact and is withdrawn.

The tracking patch was being aimed by projected geometry that disagreed with the renderer, and for four of seven objects it sat off the object entirely. Seeding from the rendered image instead moves the bowl to 0.062 ± 0.151 — not a measurement — and the rubber duck's restitution from 0.125 to 0.025 ± 0.010. Section 05 has the detail. Both surviving parameters belong to the same object.

Under the standing framing — any plausible parameter that explains the video will do — this is the acceptance test, and the vase passes it. It also passed at the old, wrong value of 0.094, which is worth stating plainly: a matching simulation confirms the parameter is realisable, not that it is right.

The rubber duck does not: measured 0.025, and the simulator cannot get below 0.261.

No contact damping across a range spanning four orders of magnitude reproduces that rebound for that mesh. The recovered parameter is not physically realisable in our simulator — a genuine negative result rather than a fitting failure, and exactly the check that treating the simulator as a hard constraint exists to perform.

04The estimator was manufacturing numbers

The retired fitter searched a parameter grid for the value whose hand-weighted motion signature best matched the track. A grid always returns a best match; it never asks whether the observation constrains anything. This comparison runs both estimators over identical tracks.

05The tracker was aimed by the wrong pipeline

Seeds were produced by projecting a body centre computed as pos + vmean, the mesh's vertex mean added to its placed position, with the object's rot_z never applied. Blender places the origin at pos and rotates it. The two conventions disagree by the mesh's own centroid offset, which is near zero for a baseball and large for anything asymmetric:

Where the offset exceeds the patch half-size, the tracker was following mostly wall. The fix is not a better projection but removing the parallel pipeline: the subject is the only thing that moves between two stagings of the same object, so differencing two initial frames isolates it exactly, in the image we are about to track. Geometry conventions cannot disagree with the renderer if the renderer is the source. 18 of 28 seeds moved by more than 20 px.

06The generated video runs about 5× too slow

Our simulation finishes falling, bouncing and settling in the first quarter of a clip and then sits still, which is why the simulated pane can look inert. That is the simulation being right: a 0.18 m fall takes 4.6 frames at 24 fps. Across 90 usable drops the generated fall takes a median 5.5× longer than physics allows (quartiles 3.2–7.8×), an effective gravity near 0.3 m/s².

This splits the parameters by dimension.

Restitution is a ratio of two speeds in the same clip, so a uniform time rescale cancels and it survives. Friction is |a|/g, which is dimensional: stretch time by α and measured acceleration falls by α². Every friction number here is therefore suspect in a way the restitutions are not, and no correction is applied because we cannot yet show the same stretch applies to horizontal motion. A drop carries its own clock — known height, known g — so staging a drop and a slide in the same clip would let one calibrate the other.

07The audit that corrected itself

An earlier version of this report stated that 68 of 93 takes contained no measurable motion. That came from tracked centroids, and the tracker fails on these clips in a way its own diagnostics cannot see: points stay “visible” while sitting on the background, so a duck that visibly fell to the table measured 6 px of motion. Re-run with appearance matching, 71 of 93 takes move and only 8 are genuinely static.

The brass pot stops being a brass pot.

All 14 degraded takes are the same object, which transforms mid-clip from a lidded pot into a wide shallow bowl. No density, friction or restitution turns a pot into a bowl, so this fails even a plausibility bar. It is asset integrity, not identifiability.

08Corrections

  • Three published parameters were artefacts of off-object tracking. The wooden bowl's friction and the rubber duck's restitution do not survive; the vase's restitution moved from 0.094 to 0.033.
  • “The videos do not contain the experiment” was an instrument artefact, stated twice on tracker evidence. 71 of 93 takes move.
  • The published baseball restitution was 0.000 — no bounce at all. On validated tracks it is 0.215 ± 0.060; the point tracker was missing the rebound outright.
  • The noise floor was assumed, and assumed too low (1.50 px against a measured 2.04 px), so every earlier interval was about a third too narrow.
  • A claimed ~80% yield for drop and slide was wrong; the real drop yield is 29%. “Moves” and “yields a usable observable” are different questions.
  • A max-excursion statistic is not a displacement. Used twice — first as path length, then again as peak displacement — and wrong both times.
  • 60 fps was not the diagnosis for mass and is no longer proposed as a requirement.
  • There is no ground truth in this pipeline. Errors were once quoted against “true” densities from a hardcoded table of textbook guesses.

09Known limits

  • The rubber duck's values come from clips in which it re-forms rather than translating; appearance matching cannot fully separate “translated” from “re-rendered nearby”.
  • A prediction I could not test: I expected the baseball's backwards de/dv to flip with more data. Its drop clips were already complete in the partial run, so the fit is over identical takes and the prediction was never actually put to the test.
  • The simulated panes are composites, not renders — simulated positions drawn with real pixels, anchored to the observed starting point.
  • Collide was dropped from the expanded lab at ~24% yield, so mass ratio is not measured here at all.
