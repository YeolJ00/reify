Three parameters, three probes, one objective

Each probe sees a different parameter, and one of them — the collision — exists to break a degeneracy the others cannot. Every iteration's simulation is shown, so the fit can be watched rather than summarised.

What each parameter is

parametermeaningprobe that sees it
μ — frictiongrip against the tabletop. Visible as the ANGLE at which it starts to slide: tan θ = μ. Mass-independent.tilt
cd — contact dampingenergy removed at impact in F = max(k·pen − cd·pen·v, 0). Sets restitution — how high and how often it bounces.drop
ρ — densitymass = ρ × mesh volume. Free fall does not depend on it; contact depends only on k/m and cd/m, so a drop alone cannot separate it from damping.collide

why the collision had to be added

Contact force is max(k·pen − cd·pen·v, 0) and acceleration is F/m, so a drop identifies only the RATIOS k/m and cd/m. Damping and mass are degenerate in it — no amount of dropping separates them. A collision sees mass directly through momentum transfer to a partner of known mass.

That degeneracy was live in every earlier run here: density was pinned at 600 for every object, so what was fitted as “restitution” was really cd/m with the mass error folded in.

read honestly

Tilt is the most consistent probe — 5/6 and 4/6 signed the same way, against 3/6 and 2/6 for the drop. Collide is the loudest and among the least consistent: median |dy| 1.72 for the brass pot, three times the tilt's 0.40, at 3/6 consistency. It is contributing magnitude rather than direction, which means summing the three probes unweighted lets the noisiest one dominate the step.

That is an argument for weighting probes by their measured consistency rather than averaging them — the joint objective is only worth more than a single probe if each term carries signal, and on this evidence one of the three mostly carries noise.
